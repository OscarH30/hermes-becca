# Follow-Up Sequences

Most replies come from follow-ups, not the first email. But more emails is not the answer. Our rule is three touches over a week, then stop. Each one adds something, and each one is easy to say no to.

## How many: three touches, then stop

Industry data points in both directions (longer campaigns catch more timing windows, but spam complaints rise with every extra email and replies drop by the fourth follow-up). We choose the polite end of that range on purpose: a small, researched list, three short emails, and a clean exit. After day 7 you do not write again.

## The cadence

| Touch | Day | Words | Job |
| ----- | --- | ----- | --- |
| 1. First email | 1 | under 90 | One true, sourced fact about them, a guess at the problem, one small ask |
| 2. Short bump | 3 | under 60 | One new useful thing, then the same small ask (or a smaller one) |
| 3. Easy out | 7 | under 50 | No pitch. "Wrong person? Who should I ask?" Then stop |

Day numbers count from the first email (day 1). In a scheduler that counts days since the first send, that is 0, 2 and 6 days after it.

**Best days to start:** Tuesday to Thursday. **Best times:** 9 to 11 AM or 1 to 3 PM in their local time. **Avoid:** Monday mornings and Friday afternoons. If a touch lands on a weekend, send it the next working day.

**Any reply stops everything.** A yes, a no, a question, an angry note, from anyone at that company. Check for a reply, a stop request, or a bounce before each touch. A stop request goes on the do-not-contact list the same day.

## What each touch does

### Touch 2 (day 3): add one new useful thing

Pick one, and only one:

- A second true observation about their business, with a source.
- A short general pattern ("businesses with a second location often...").
- One specific idea for their business, in a sentence or two.

Then repeat the same small ask, or a smaller one. Only use a customer story or result that is in `brain/business.md`. If you have no new true thing, write "no new fact" for that prospect and tell the owner. Do not invent one.

> One more thing on [Company]: [one new, true, useful thing in one or two short sentences].
>
> [Same small ask.]

### Touch 3 (day 7): the easy out

No pitch. Give them a way to point you to the right person or to say no. People answer when no is easy.

> I haven't heard back, so this may not be for you, or it may not be the right time. If someone else at [Company] looks after [the area], who should I ask?
>
> If not, no problem. I'll leave it here.

A shorter version for a more direct voice:

> Is [the area] something you look after, or should I be talking to someone else? Either answer is fine. After this I won't write again.

Subject for touch 3 can be `wrong person` or `right person?`. For touches 2 and 3, give a new email a plain 2 to 3 word lowercase subject. If your sending tool threads the follow-up under the first email, that is fine. Never type "Re:" or "Fwd:" yourself.

### After day 7

Stop. No fourth email, no "one last try." Come back only if they reply, or if the owner decides there is a new, real reason at least 90 days later.

## Phrases that kill response rates

- "I never heard back" lowers meeting bookings (Gong)
- "Just checking in" has zero value and signals laziness
- "Bumping this to the top of your inbox" is presumptuous
- "Did you see my last email?" is guilt-tripping
- "Following up on my previous message" adds nothing

Also avoid guilt ("I'm surprised I haven't heard back"), fake urgency, and repeating the first email with the same words.

## Same rules on every touch

Plain text. No links, images, or attachments. Owner's signature, real business address, and an opt-out line ("If this is not relevant, reply 'stop' and I won't email you again"). No invented facts. Approve all three touches for a prospect together, before the first goes out.

## CTA adjustment by seniority

**Executives and founders:** ultra-low-effort, curiosity-driven. "Curious?" or "Worth a quick look?"

**Mid-level managers:** a bit more specific. "Want me to send two ideas for your setup?"

Higher in the org chart means less friction you can ask for.
